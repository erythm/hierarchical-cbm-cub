\begin{abstract}\small
The goal of this project is \emph{explainability-by-design} for fine-grained
image classification: instead of trusting a black-box CNN/Transformer, we
want every prediction to be readable as a sequence of human-understandable
visual concepts. We take a standard image backbone -- in our case
\textbf{ResNet-50} or \textbf{ViT-B/16} -- and, instead of feeding its
penultimate feature vector directly into a 200-way classifier, we
\emph{interpose two additional concept layers} between the feature vector
and the final decision. The first added layer maps the feature vector to
\textbf{13 coarse body-part probabilities} (e.g.\ bill, wing, tail, belly);
the second layer maps the same feature vector to \textbf{312 fine
attributes} (e.g.\ \emph{bill colour: rufous}, \emph{wing pattern:
striped}). The two layers are explicitly coupled by a multiplicative
\emph{parent-conditioned gate}: each of the 312 fine attributes is gated by
the probability of its parent body part, identically at training and
inference, so the hierarchy is enforced architecturally rather than by a
soft penalty. The final classifier is a single linear layer
$W\!:\!\mathbb{R}^{312}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}^{200}$ that consumes \emph{only}
these 312 gated concepts. Every species prediction is therefore forced to
be a transparent function of the bottleneck: for any image we can read off
which body parts the model detected, which fine attributes were activated
inside each part, and exactly which attributes pushed the predicted class.
We use both ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16 in parallel to show that this
explainable design transfers across very different feature extractors --
the same kind of explanation comes out on top of either backbone -- and we
evaluate the resulting explanations on CUB-200-2011 along two axes:
\emph{concept quality} (per-attribute AUC -- are the concepts correctly
detected?) and \emph{concept necessity} (per-part ablation -- does
removing a concept group actually change the decision?).
\end{abstract}

\section{Goal -- explainability by design}\label{sec:goal}
A standard image classifier $\hat y = \mathrm{argmax}\,W f$ feeds the
penultimate feature vector $f$ of a CNN or Transformer directly into a
softmax layer. The vector $f$ has no human meaning -- there is no way to
read \emph{why} a particular species was chosen. The goal of this project
is to remove that opacity at the architectural level: instead of letting
$f$ go straight into the classifier, we route it through two extra layers
that are themselves human-understandable concepts, and force the linear
classifier to consume \emph{only those concepts}. The resulting model
$\hat y = \mathrm{argmax}\,W\,p_f$ is exactly as expressive as the
classifier we trust the linear bottleneck to allow, and exactly as
explainable as the 312 concepts it now consumes.

\section{Hierarchical architecture}\label{sec:arch}
The pipeline has four stages, organised strictly top-down.

\subsection{Stage 1 -- backbone (feature extractor)}
We use a standard image backbone in its frozen, off-the-shelf form: either
ResNet-50 (336\,px input, ImageNet-V2 weights) or ViT-B/16 (384\,px,
SWAG-E2E weights). The backbone produces a single dense feature vector
$f$ (2048-d for ResNet-50, 768-d for ViT-B/16). $f$ is \emph{not} the
explanation -- it is just the raw representation that the two added layers
will translate into concepts.

\subsection{Stage 2 -- first added layer (13 body parts)}
A small head $W_c$ projects $f$ to 13 probabilities, one per body part:
\[
p_c \;=\; \sigma(W_c f + b_c)\,, \quad p_c\in[0,1]^{13},
\]
with parts $\{$\texttt{back, belly, bill, breast, crown, eye, forehead,
head, leg, nape, tail, throat, wing}$\}$. Reading $p_c$ already answers ``which body parts is the model looking at?''.

\subsection{Stage 3 -- second added layer (312 attributes with parent gate)}
A second head $W_f$ projects $[f \,;\, p_c]$ to 312 raw fine logits, gated
by their parent body part:
\begin{equation}
p_f[i] \;=\; \sigma(z_f[i]) \cdot \bigl(0.5 + 0.5\,p_c[\mathrm{parent}(i)]\bigr).
\label{eq:mask}
\end{equation}
Eq.~(\ref{eq:mask}) enforces the hierarchy architecturally: low parent
probability damps every child attribute (floor $0.5$, never zero, so
gradients survive); high parent lets the child fire fully. The 34
global attributes (e.g.\ \texttt{shape}, \texttt{size}) use mask $=1$.
The gate is \emph{identical} at training and inference, so the classifier
never sees out-of-distribution $p_f$ values.

\subsection{Stage 4 -- linear classifier on the bottleneck}
\[
\hat y \;=\; \mathrm{argmax}\;\bigl(W\,p_f + b\bigr),
\quad W \in \mathbb{R}^{200\times 312}.
\]
There is no residual side-channel from $f$ to the classifier; the 312
gated concepts are a \emph{sufficient statistic} for the prediction.

\section{Training}\label{sec:train}
The 13/312 hierarchy is parsed deterministically from the CUB attribute
names; no extra annotation is required. Part labels are the OR-aggregation
of their children. The model is trained in three sequential phases:
phase~1 trains both heads on a frozen backbone with a loss
$\lambda_c L_{\text{coarse}} + \lambda_f L_{\text{fine}} +
0.1\,L_{\text{task}}$ (weighted BCE / Focal Loss / CE-LS); phase~2
calibrates \emph{only} the linear classifier on the model's own
\emph{predicted}~$p_f$ (so the head learns the actual distribution it will
see at evaluation, not a clean GT distribution); phase~3 jointly
fine-tunes everything with concept-dominant weights ($2/2/1$) and MixUp
$\alpha{=}0.2$. The backbone uses an order-of-magnitude lower learning
rate than the heads, and for ViT-SWAG the backbone rate is reduced
further to preserve attention features.

\section{Reading a single prediction}\label{sec:walkthrough}
Because the classifier is a single linear layer over 312 concepts, every
prediction can be reduced to a fully explicit causal chain
(Fig.~\ref{fig:walkthrough}): the backbone produces $f$; the first added
layer turns $f$ into $p_c$ (which parts are visible); the second added
layer turns $f$ and $p_c$ into the gated $p_f$ (which fine attributes are
present); and the predicted class is the one whose row in $W$ aligns best
with that $p_f$. To explain a specific prediction we simply rank the
contributions $W[\hat y, i]\cdot p_f[i]$ for $i=1\dots 312$ and read off
the top contributors. There is no probing, no surrogate model, no
post-hoc attribution: the explanation \emph{is} the decision.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/walkthrough.png}
\caption{Reading a single prediction. From top to bottom: input image;
13 body-part probabilities $p_c$; fine attributes inside the most
active part, gated through Eq.~(\ref{eq:mask}); top contributions
$W[\hat y, i]\cdot p_f[i]$ to the predicted class. The whole decision
is visible on this single figure.}
\label{fig:walkthrough}
\end{figure}

\section{How well are the 312 concepts learned?}\label{sec:concepts}
The explanation in Section~\ref{sec:walkthrough} is only trustworthy if
the 312 attribute detectors themselves are accurate. For each attribute
we therefore compute its AUC against the CUB ground-truth attribute
labels on the test split, and look at the 10 best- and 10 worst-detected
attributes for each backbone.

\subsection{ResNet-50 instantiation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:auc_resnet} shows the AUC ranking for the ResNet-50
H-CBM. The best-detected attributes (left) are visually obvious
yellow body-part colours -- both because yellow is salient and because
it is well represented in CUB -- and reach $\mathrm{AUC}\geq 0.87$. The
worst-detected attributes (right) gather rare colours
(\texttt{pink}/\texttt{purple} body parts), the rare colour
\texttt{rufous} and unusual shapes (\texttt{owl-like},
\texttt{very\_large}); their AUC sits in the $0.36$--$0.49$ band,
i.e.\ barely above chance. The take-away for the explanation is
direct: a prediction whose top contributors are yellow body colours
is well-supported by the bottleneck, while a prediction that hinges
on a rare colour or shape attribute should be read with caution.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/attr_auc_resnet.png}
\caption{Per-attribute AUC of the ResNet-50 H-CBM on the test split.
\textbf{Left:} the 10 attributes the model detects most reliably
(yellow body-part colours, AUC $\geq 0.87$). \textbf{Right:} the
10 attributes the model detects worst (rare pink/purple colours,
rare shapes, AUC $\leq 0.49$). Higher AUC means the predicted
attribute probability genuinely ranks images that have the
attribute above images that do not.}
\label{fig:auc_resnet}
\end{figure}

\subsection{ViT-B/16 instantiation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:auc_vit} shows the same analysis for the ViT-B/16
H-CBM. The best-detected concepts are again the yellow body-part
colours; the worst-detected concepts are again concentrated on rare
colours. The structure of the explanation is therefore the
\emph{same shape} on both backbones, which is what we want from an
explainable-by-design pipeline: the readable side of the model does
not depend on which feature extractor was bolted under it.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/attr_auc_vit.png}
\caption{Per-attribute AUC of the ViT-B/16 H-CBM on the test split,
same axes as Fig.~\ref{fig:auc_resnet}. The set of easiest and
hardest concepts is recovered \emph{independently} by a completely
different backbone, which means the explanation pattern is a property
of the hierarchical bottleneck design rather than of a particular
feature extractor.}
\label{fig:auc_vit}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Concept controllability}\label{sec:tti}
Faithfulness of the bottleneck can be tested directly: replace a fraction $r$
of the 312 predicted concept probabilities with ground-truth values and re-run
the linear classifier. At $r{=}0\%$ we get the normal baseline; at
$r{=}25\%$, $50\%$, $75\%$, and $r{=}100\%$ accuracy climbs monotonically
toward the oracle (Fig.~\ref{fig:tti}). There is no hidden residual
side-channel -- fixing more concepts always helps.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/tti_curve.png}
\caption{Concept-intervention curve for both backbones. At $r{=}0\%$
we recover the baseline (ResNet: 61.9\%, ViT: 86.6\%); as $r$ grows
accuracy climbs monotonically toward the oracle (ResNet: 96.9\%,
ViT: 100.0\%). Both curves confirm that the linear classifier
genuinely uses the concept layer.}
\label{fig:tti}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Difficulty distribution across parts}\label{sec:partcomp}
Fig.~\ref{fig:partcomp} shows how the 312 attributes split into difficulty
tiers (Easy: AUC$\geq$0.75 on both backbones; Very hard: neither) across the
13 body parts. \texttt{tail} has the most Very-hard attributes ($n{=}14$) yet
is also the most causally necessary -- a clear direction for future work.

\section{Are the concept groups causally necessary?}\label{sec:necessity}
A faithful explanation should not just be a list of concepts that
\emph{correlate} with the prediction -- it should pinpoint the
concepts the model genuinely \emph{needs} to make the decision. We
test this by zeroing the $p_f$ columns of one body part at a time
across the whole test split, re-running the linear classifier, and
recording the resulting drop in accuracy. Because the classifier
consumes $p_f$ directly, this is a clean causal counterfactual:
``what would the model do if it could not see this body part?''

\subsection{ResNet-50 instantiation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:need_resnet} (left) shows the result for ResNet-50. The
three body parts the model relies on most heavily are
\texttt{bill}, \texttt{tail} and \texttt{belly}: zeroing
\texttt{bill} alone drops accuracy by 1.4~pp, and the top three
together account for more than half of the total ablation budget.
The tail of the ranking (\texttt{crown}, \texttt{forehead},
\texttt{nape}) contributes less than 0.2~pp each.

\subsection{ViT-B/16 instantiation}
The same ablation on ViT-B/16 (Fig.~\ref{fig:need_resnet}, right)
recovers the same three parts in the same order with very similar
magnitudes. The \emph{which parts matter} signal is a property of
the H-CBM design, not of the backbone.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conc}
We presented a hierarchical concept bottleneck model whose entire
purpose is explainability: by inserting two human-understandable
concept layers (13 body parts and 312 gated fine attributes)
between any image backbone and the final linear classifier, we
turn every prediction into a readable causal chain. We ran the
same explainable design on top of two very different feature
extractors (ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16); on both, the explanation
surfaces the same way -- the same yellow body colours come out as
the easiest concepts, the same rare colours and shapes come out
as the hardest, and the same three body parts (\texttt{bill},
\texttt{tail}, \texttt{belly}) come out as causally necessary.
The H-CBM bottleneck is therefore an explainability layer that
sits on top of \emph{any} backbone, not a property of one
particular architecture.
