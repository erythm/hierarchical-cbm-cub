Trustworthy AI:
Motivation & Definitions

Eliana Pastor · Explainable and Trustworthy AI

B

The Need for Trustworthy AI — Regulatory Context

◆ Society increasingly relies on AI for critical decisions: healthcare, finance, justice, and more.
◆ EU AI Act — First binding regulation specifically on artificial intelligence.
◆ GDPR — Arts. 13 & 14: right to "meaningful information about the logic involved" in profiling. Art. 22: right not to be subject to purely automated decisions with legal effects.
◆ EU Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI — European Commission framework defining 7 key requirements.
C

7 Key Requirements for Trustworthy AI

Source: European Commission — Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI

1 Transparency & Explainability

Most AI models are black boxes. Opaqueness exists at multiple levels: data, model/algorithm, learned patterns, and business model. Transparency is achieved through three pillars:

  • Explainability: Explain reasoning in understandable terms, tailored to stakeholder type (layperson, expert, regulator, researcher). Manage the accuracy–explainability trade-off.
  • Traceability: Document data sets, labelling, algorithms, and processes. Facilitates auditability.
  • Communication: Inform users of capabilities, limitations, and risks. Humans have the right to know they are interacting with an AI system.

Skin cancer AI example: technical explanations for clinicians, simplified explanations for patients, disclosure that AI is in use.

2 Technical Robustness & Safety

AI must be resilient, secure, and minimize unintended harm. Four sub-requirements:

  • General Safety: Define risk metrics, assess threats, maintain a fallback plan (rule-based or human-operator backup).
  • Resilience to Attack & Security: Guard against data poisoning, model leakage, model inversion, and adversarial/evasion attacks. Monitor continuously.
  • Accuracy: Use up-to-date, high-quality, complete, representative data. Indicate error likelihood. Critical applications require high accuracy thresholds.
  • Reliability & Reproducibility: Consistent results across inputs and repeated runs under identical conditions. Document the full pipeline.
3 Fairness, Diversity & Non-Discrimination

Data reflects societal biases. ML can perpetuate and amplify historical discrimination.

  • Avoid Unfair Bias: Identify and remove bias at data collection, processing, and algorithm design stages. Define clear fairness metrics and evaluate across multiple groups.
  • Representativeness: Ensure data reflects demographic diversity.
  • Universal Design: Systems usable regardless of age, gender, ability, or characteristics.
  • Diverse Expertise: Include professionals from diverse backgrounds in the development team.
4 Accountability

Accountability is distributed across all actors:

  • AI Users: Understand functionality and limitations; ensure appropriate use.
  • Businesses: Establish clear guidelines; accountable for organizational consequences.
  • AI Developers: Design and train responsibly with safety measures.
  • Data Providers: Accountable for quality and accuracy of training data.
  • Auditability: Internal and external auditors assess algorithms, data, and design. Enabled by traceability and logging.
5 Privacy & Data Governance

Privacy is a fundamental right. AI may infer sensitive attributes (preferences, orientation, religion, political views).

  • Privacy: Assess impact at every lifecycle stage. Guarantee right to be forgotten. Prevent unlawful discrimination via collected data.
  • Data Governance: Manage quality, integrity, security, and availability. Document data at every stage. Follow GDPR and applicable regulations.
6 Human Agency & Oversight

AI must support — not replace — human decision-making. GDPR Art. 22: right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing.

  • Human-in-the-Loop (HITL): Human intervention required in every individual decision cycle.
  • Human-on-the-Loop (HOTL): Human intervenes during system design and monitors ongoing operation.
  • Human-in-Command (HIC): Humans oversee overall AI activity and decide when and how to use it.
7 Societal & Environmental Well-Being
  • AI must benefit all human beings, including future generations.
  • Sustainability: Assess resource usage and energy consumption during training.
  • Societal Impact: Consider effects on human work, institutions, and democracy. Minimize societal harm.
  • Avoid widening healthcare, economic, or social disparities.
A

Why Trust Matters — Real-World Failures

ML models are pervasive: Medical Diagnosis · Recommender Systems · Social Networks · Finance · Legal · Smart Cities

Case 1 Predicting Pneumonia Risk
  • Goal: predict death risk in pneumonia patients. Two models: interpretable (less accurate) vs. black-box (more accurate). Researchers chose the interpretable one.
  • The interpretable model revealed a spurious pattern: asthma → lower death risk. Counterintuitive — asthmatics receive earlier, higher-quality care, reducing mortality. Deploying this rule directly would be fatal.
  • The black-box model would have hidden this pattern entirely. In high-risk domains, experts must inspect the model before deployment.
  • Caruana et al., KDD 2015.
Case 2 COMPAS — Predicting Recidivism
  • COMPAS: risk-assessment tool used by US judges to score likelihood of re-offending.
  • ProPublica analyzed 7,000 people arrested in Broward County, FL (2013–14).
  • Finding: African-American defendants wrongly assigned high-risk at almost twice the rate of white defendants. White defendants mislabeled low-risk more often than Black defendants.
  • propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing
Case 3 AI Recruiting Tool — Gender Bias
  • AI recruiting tool penalized applicants from all-women's colleges and CVs containing the word "women's" (e.g., "women's chess club").
  • Root cause: trained on historical hiring data from a male-dominated industry — it learned and perpetuated existing biases.
Case 4 Models Can Be Fooled — Adversarial Attacks
  • Image perturbation (2015): Imperceptible noise caused a CNN to classify a panda as a gibbon with 99%+ confidence. (Goodfellow et al., ICLR 2015)
  • Adversarial patch: A printed patch caused YOLOv2 person detector to ignore people holding it — exploitable to evade surveillance. (Thys et al., CVPR 2019)
Case 5 Accountability — Air Canada Chatbot
  • Air Canada's chatbot gave a customer false information about the refund policy.
  • Airline argued the chatbot is "a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions." Court rejected this — ruled in favor of the customer (partial refund + court fees). Chatbot was shut down.
Case 6 Historical Bias — UK Exam Grade Algorithm (2020)
  • During COVID-19, A-levels were replaced by algorithmic predictions. Algorithm accused of bias against students from poorer backgrounds.
  • Central problem: lack of transparency — no one understood how predictions were made, destroying public trust.
Case 7 Apple Card — Gender Discrimination?
  • Husband and wife with same credit history applied for Apple Card. Husband received a credit limit 20x higher, sparking discrimination accusations.
  • After investigation: no direct bias found in protected attributes, but inputs were not identical. Trust was damaged and extremely difficult to rebuild.
Case 8 Google Gemini — Not Easy to Make Models Fair
  • Google blocked Gemini's image-generation of people after anti-White bias accusations in historical images.
  • Statement: "We did not want Gemini to refuse to create images of any particular group. And we did not want it to create inaccurate historical images." Balancing fairness constraints is genuinely hard.

Explainable AI:
Taxonomy

Eliana Pastor · Explainable and Trustworthy AI

A

Definition of Explainability / Interpretability

Core Definition

The ability to explain or to present the reasoning behind the decisions of AI systems or its technical processes in understandable terms to humans. Focus on humans as the targets of explainability.

Most AI models are black boxes. Opaqueness can exist at multiple levels: Data — Model/Algorithm — Learned function/patterns — Business model of the AI product.

Terminology Multiple Terms — Not a Monolithic Concept

No agreement within the ML community on a single definition. Terms are often used interchangeably, but carry subtle distinctions:

  • Interpretability: A model is transparent in its operation and shows relationships between inputs and outputs. Focuses more on models that are inherently interpretable.
  • Explainability: Ability to explain the decision-making process in terms understandable to end users. Focuses more on models that are by design incomprehensible — providing an explanation after the fact.
  • Understandability: A model that can be understood in a reasonable amount of time.
  • Comprehensibility: Emphasis as enabler to produce knowledge — enabled when patterns identified by AI are comprehensible.
  • Intelligibility: A model is intelligible if it is interpretable by humans.
  • Mental Fit: A human's ability to grasp and internalize the model.

Convention used in this course: Interpretability → interpretable-by-design models. Explainability → explaining pre-trained models to make them interpretable.

B

Desiderata of Explainability Research

By understanding an AI model and its predictions, we also achieve other desiderata — which are also requirements for Trustworthy AI:

◆ Trust: If we can understand the model, we can decide whether to trust it or not. (e.g., pneumonia risk case: interpretability revealed an unsafe pattern; Apple Card: opacity prevented users from trusting the model.)
◆ Fairness & Ethical Decision-Making: Understanding the model lets us assess whether it relies on sensitive or protected attributes. (e.g., COMPAS: analysis revealed biased predictions; UK exams: opacity created unresolvable fairness concerns.)
◆ Robustness: Inspecting erroneous predictions enables active model debugging.
◆ Informativeness: Revealing reasons behind predictions informs users. (e.g., loan rejection: "We rejected your loan because your income was insufficient or unstable.")
◆ Knowledge: Inspecting model behavior can yield genuinely new knowledge. (e.g., AlphaGo: "It's not a human move. I've never seen a human play this move. So beautiful." — Fan Hui, European Go Champion.)
C

A Taxonomy of Explainable AI

Five Dimensions of Classification

At which step of the ML pipeline?
  • Pre-modeling, In-modeling, Post-modeling
Is the method general?
  • Model-dependent vs. Model-agnostic
What do we explain?
  • Global model, subgroups, or a local/individual prediction
How are explanations represented?
  • Feature importance, rules, visualizations, examples
How are explanations derived?
  • Explaining by removing/perturbing, local surrogates, gradient-based, counterfactual methods
D

Stages of Explainability

Explainability involves the entire AI development pipeline, not just model output.

Stage 1 Pre-Modeling Explainability

Before the actual modeling process. Goal: understand the data and preprocess it while preserving understandability. May include assessing and addressing biases in data.

  • Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA): Extract a summary of the main characteristics of a dataset.
  • Interpretable Feature Engineering: Selection and preprocessing of features that preserves their interpretability.
  • Data Description & Summarization: Standardize documentation; ensure proper communication between data providers and dataset users.
Stage 2 Explainable Modeling (In-Modeling)

Design, train, and adopt more interpretable or explainable models. Three approaches:

  • Inherently Explainable Models: Trees, linear models, GAMs, GA2Ms, GLMs, decision sets/rules, concept-based models. Note: using these does not automatically guarantee explainability (e.g., deep trees or linear models on high-dimensional data can still be opaque). Trade-off: interpretable models are typically less accurate.
  • Interpretability by Design: Design high-performing models with interpretability constraints (e.g., regularization on number of tree leaves or number of non-zero weights in a linear model). These may still underperform compared to unconstrained complex models.
  • Explanations-in-the-Loop: Train AI systems to jointly produce a prediction and its explanation. Example: TED (Teaching Explanations for Decisions) framework — trains using ground-truth explanations (user rationales). Drawbacks: requires annotated explanations; explanations may reflect human expectations rather than actual model reasoning → tension between faithfulness (reflects model) and plausibility (satisfies humans).
Stage 3 Post-Modeling Explainability (Post-Hoc)

Generate explanations for pre-trained models. Most high-performing models are black boxes that optimize only for performance. The majority of XAI approaches focus here, addressing interpretability concerns for pre-trained black-box models.

Post-hoc approaches differentiate along the other taxonomy dimensions: generalizability, scope, representation, and derivation method.

E

Generalizability of Explainability

Model-Dependent
  • Only applicable to specific model types.
  • Relies on model structure and internal properties.
  • e.g., specific approaches for SVMs, or for a particular neural network architecture.
Model-Agnostic
  • Applicable to any model.
  • Treats the model as an oracle — uses only predictions and output probabilities.
  • Does not require access to model internals.
F

Scope of Explainability

Global
Describes the overall behavior of the model. Explains how the model works in general. Examples: global surrogate models, partial dependence plots, permutation feature importance.
Subgroup
Explains how the model behaves for specific subsets or segments of the data, rather than globally or for a single instance.
Individual / Local
Explains individual predictions. Simpler task than global explanation. Easier to understand and analyze. Multiple approaches differ in representation and derivation.
G

Explanation Representation

Type 1 Feature Importance (Input Attribution)

Indicates how much each feature contributed to the prediction for a given instance. Applicable to structured data, text, and images. Presented numerically, graphically, or in tabular format.

Type 2 Rule Explanations

Local rules that provide insights into how a model behaves for a specific instance. Often extracted from local surrogate models. Presented as logical statements, decision paths, or rules.

If Country = US AND married AND work_hours > 45 → Income > 50K

Type 3 Visualization-Based Explanations

Use visual representations to explain model behavior. Example: Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) plots — show dependence between the target response and an input feature of interest for individual instances.

Type 4 Explanations by Example

Select or generate specific instances to explain model behavior:

  • Prototypes: Representative instance(s) of the prediction class.
  • Counterfactuals: The slightest change to the input that changes the prediction. (e.g., "Loan approved if income increases by 10K.")
  • Adversarial Examples: Counterfactuals where small changes are applied to fool the model rather than interpret it.
H

Methodology to Derive Explanations

Method 1 Explaining by Removing / Perturbation-Based

Also known as occlusion-based. The idea: remove or simulate the removal of one or more input features, then measure the change in model output to quantify each feature's influence.

f(y=c | x) ≠ f(y=c | x \ {gender=female, nation=Italy}) → these features are influential

Method 2 Local Surrogate Interpretable Model

Approximate the behavior of complex black-box models in the locality of a specific prediction. Train an interpretable model (decision tree, linear model) on data points in the vicinity of the instance to be explained. Provides insights into black-box behavior in the neighborhood of a specific data point.

Ribeiro et al., "Why should I trust you?", KDD 2016 — Guidotti et al., Local rule-based explanations, 2018

Method 3 Gradient-Based Explanation Methods

Leverage gradients of the model's output with respect to input features. Quantify how changes in input features affect the model's predictions by computing partial derivatives. Most applicable to differentiable models (e.g., neural networks).

Method 4 Counterfactual Methods

Generate alternative instances (counterfactuals) with similar characteristics to the original input but different model predictions. Used to understand how small changes in input features affect the model's output.

Loan rejected → Loan approved  |  If income increases of 10K

Pre-Modeling
Explainability

Eliana Pastor · Explainable and Trustworthy AI

A

Overview — Pre-Modeling Explainability

Before the actual modeling process. Explainability involves the entire AI development pipeline, not only the model output.

◆ Goal 1: Gain more useful insights from data and use them for model development.
◆ Goal 2: Preprocess data for model development while preserving understandability.

Three Components

  • Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA): Extract a summary of the main characteristics of the data.
  • Dataset Description & Summarization: Standardize documentation; ensure proper communication between data creators and users.
  • Interpretable Feature Engineering: Selection and preprocessing of features while preserving their interpretability.
B

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

Statistical techniques and visualizations to gain insights into a dataset.

What EDA Does
  • Extract a summary of the data structure and content.
  • Visualize the dataset distribution and relationships.
  • Compute and analyze statistical properties: mean, standard deviation, percentage of missing samples, feature dimensionality, presence of outliers.
  • Expose biases that might exist within the data before modeling.
  • Enable a better understanding of what the model will learn from such data.
Tool 1 ydata-profiling
  • Univariate analysis: Descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, etc.) and visualizations for each feature.
  • Multivariate analysis: Correlations, missing data, pairwise interactions between features.
  • Dataset comparison: Fast and complete report comparing two datasets.
  • github.com/ydataai/ydata-profiling
Tool 2 FACETS
  • Visual feature-by-feature statistical analysis.
  • Data distribution with focus on common issues such as missing feature values.
  • Exploration of relationships between data points across different features.
  • pair-code.github.io/facets/

Other common libraries: NumPy, Pandas, Scikit-learn. Ad-hoc tools: Tableau, KNIME.

C

Dataset Description & Summarization

Goals of Dataset Documentation
  • Communication: Ensure proper exchange between data creators and users.
  • Transparency: Clear data origin, characteristics, and potential biases. Users understand how data was collected, processed, and analyzed.
  • Avoid Misuse: Prevent inappropriate use of the dataset.
  • Ethical Considerations: Help address systemic bias in models developed on the data.
  • Reproducibility: Enable reproduction of results or analyses conducted with the data.
  • Data Governance: Provide guidelines and standards for data management practices.
  • Collaboration & Sharing: Documented data can be easily shared and understood by different users.
  • Long-Term Preservation: Ensures data remains accessible and usable over time by maintaining structural, format, and metadata information.
  • Risk Management: Identify potential risks such as privacy concerns, security vulnerabilities, or data quality issues.
Points to Address in Dataset Documentation
  • Motivation: Reasons for creating the dataset; who created or funded it.
  • Composition: What the dataset provides; presence of errors, noise, or redundancies.
  • Collection Process: How data was acquired; who was involved in collection and review.
  • Preprocessing / Cleansing / Tagging: Info on preprocessing steps or software used, so users can determine compatibility with their intended use.
  • Uses: Tasks for which the data may or may not be used.
  • Distribution: How the dataset will be disseminated; restrictions and licenses.
  • Maintenance: Planned updates, support channels, and communication to users.
Standardization Frameworks
  • Datasheets for Datasets: Gebru, T., et al. Communications of the ACM 64.12 (2021).
  • Data Statements: Bender, E. and Friedman, B. Toward Mitigating System Bias and Enabling Better Science. TACL, 2018.
  • Dataset Nutrition Labels: Holland, Sarah, et al. Data Protection and Privacy 12.12 (2020): 1. — A visual label summarizing key dataset properties (analogous to food nutrition labels).
D

Interpretable Feature Engineering

Selection and preprocessing of features that preserve their interpretability.

Feature Selection

Using a lower number of features reduces complexity and makes the process and model easier to interpret.

  • Recursive Feature Elimination: Iteratively remove least important features.
  • Domain Expert-Driven: Experts select the most important features for the problem.
  • Correlation-Based: Compute correlations among features; keep only one representative from groups of highly correlated features.
Interpretable Feature Transformation

Creating or transforming features in a way that makes them humanly understandable:

  • Discretization: Convert continuous values into ranges. (e.g., age → <30, 30–60, >60)
  • Semantic Binning: Convert into meaningful labels. (e.g., age → young, adult, senior)
  • Statistics over Windows: From time series to interpretable summaries. (e.g., mean, percentiles, standard deviation of time windows)
  • Domain Knowledge Integration: Create domain-driven features that are meaningful for the problem and naturally interpretable.

In-Modeling
Explainability

Eliana Pastor · Explainable and Trustworthy AI

A

Overview — Explainable Modeling

Design, train, and adopt more interpretable/explainable models. Three main approaches:

◆ Inherently Explainable Models: Adopt models that are transparent by nature (trees, rules, linear models, etc.). Note: does not automatically guarantee explainability for all configurations.
◆ Interpretability by Design: Design high-performing models with explicit interpretability constraints (e.g., regularization).
◆ Explanations-in-the-Loop: Train AI systems to jointly produce a prediction and its explanation.

Key tension: interpretable models are typically less accurate — the accuracy–explainability trade-off.

B

Decision Trees

Structure
  • Root Node: Topmost node where the first decision is made.
  • Inner/Decision Nodes: Represent decisions or tests on a feature attribute.
  • Edges: Possible outcomes of a decision (yes/no, value ranges).
  • Leaf Nodes: Terminal nodes that provide the final prediction/class label.
Building Algorithm
  • Begin with the entire dataset at the root node.
  • Select the best splitting attribute and value based on a splitting criterion (e.g., Gini Impurity).
  • Partition the dataset into subsets based on the selected attribute's values.
  • Recursively apply steps 2–3 to each subset until: all instances belong to the same class, no more attributes to split on, or stopping criteria are met (max depth, min samples per leaf).
  • Assign the majority class label to each leaf node.
Global Interpretability
  • Tree Structure: Analyze the full decision paths across the tree.
  • Decision Rules: Extract if–then rules from tree paths (e.g., width < 0.8 → setosa). Some users find rules more readable than the tree itself.
  • Feature Importance: Three methods — Impurity-Based (Gini importance: normalized total reduction in Gini index per feature), Depth-Based (features closer to root are more important), Path-Based (features appearing more frequently have higher importance).

Impurity-based importance: for each split, measure the weighted reduction in Gini index vs. the parent node. Sum across all splits, normalize to [0,1].

Local Interpretability

Path Explanation: Trace the decision path from root to the leaf node for a specific instance.

Instance: petal_width=1.1, petal_length=5.0
Path: petal_width<0.8 = False → petal_width<1.7 = True → petal_length<4.9 = False → virginica

Advantages
  • Global, local, and subgroup interpretability.
  • Human-friendly explanations; simple to read.
  • Built-in visualization enhances understanding.
  • Facilitates communication with non-technical stakeholders.
  • Users can assess whether to trust the model.
Limitations
  • Lower accuracy than complex models (accuracy–explainability trade-off).
  • Only interpretable when small (low depth, few splits).
  • Large trees are as opaque as black-box models.
C

Decision Rules

Definition

Classify instances using if…then… rules. A rule has the form: (Condition) → y, where Condition is a conjunction of simple predicates and y is the class label.

  • Rule Induction Algorithms: CN2, RIPPER — explicitly generate rules from training data.
  • Associative Classifiers: Rules derived from association rule mining.
  • From Decision Trees: Extract rules by enumerating root-to-leaf paths.
Two Variants
  • Decision List: Ordered rules. Prediction based on the first rule that satisfies the instance.
  • Decision Set: Independent, mutually exclusive rules. Conflicts resolved by majority voting or other strategies. (Lakkaraju et al., KDD 2016)
Interpretability
  • Global: Analyze the rule set as a whole; features appearing in multiple rules are more important.
  • Local: Analyze the single rule that fires for a given instance.
  • Subgroup: Each rule covers a subset of the data.
Advantages
  • Global, local, and subgroup interpretability.
  • More compact and expressive than trees.
  • Some users find rules more interpretable than tree structures.
  • Human-friendly; facilitates communication.
Limitations
  • Often require categorical data (numerical features must be discretized).
  • Lower accuracy than complex models.
  • Only interpretable when compact (few, short rules).
D

Linear & Logistic Regression

Linear Regression

y = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + … + βₙxₙ

  • Each coefficient βᵢ represents the change in the dependent variable for a one-unit increase in xᵢ, holding all other variables constant.
  • Positive βᵢ: as xᵢ increases, y increases. Negative βᵢ: as xᵢ increases, y decreases.
  • Intercept β₀: predicted value when all independent variables are zero (may not be meaningful if zero has no practical interpretation).

Example: Salary = 40,000 + 3,000 × YearsExperience + 2,000 × YearsEducation. Each additional year of experience adds $3,000 to salary, holding education constant.

Logistic Regression

P(y=1) = 1 / (1 + exp(−(β₀ + β₁x₁ + … + βₙxₙ)))

  • Coefficients represent the change in log odds for a one-unit change in the predictor, holding others constant.
  • Increasing feature xᵢ by one unit changes the odds by a factor of exp(βᵢ).

Example: Exam pass prediction. β₁=0.8 (hours studied) → each additional study hour multiplies the odds of passing by e^0.8 ≈ 2.22. β₂=1.2 (prep course) → attending multiplies odds by e^1.2 ≈ 3.32.

Advantages
  • Simple interpretation via coefficients.
  • Provide variable importance (magnitude and sign of coefficients).
Limitations
  • Lower accuracy than complex models.
  • Limited to linear decision boundaries.
  • Interpretability decreases as the number of coefficients grows.
E

Other Inherently Explainable Models

Naive Bayes
  • Feature Importance: Given by the conditional probabilities of features given class labels. Higher P(feature | class) → more indicative of that class.
  • Advantage: Simple, easy to implement, provides feature importance.
  • Limitations: Assumes feature independence (rarely true in practice); limited expressiveness and low performance on complex tasks.
KNN (K-Nearest Neighbors)
  • Prediction based on the K nearest neighbors of the instance — explanation by example.
  • Inherently local: does NOT offer global interpretability.
  • Advantages: Easy to derive explanations; example-based reasoning is close to how humans reason; intuitive for data types like images.
  • Limitations: Difficult to interpret as the number of features increases; other forms of explanation (feature importance) may be preferred.
F

Interpretability by Design

Design high-performing models with explicit interpretability constraints, rather than using inherently simple models.

Approach Regularization for Explainability
  • Apply regularization to constrain model complexity: e.g., limit the number of leaves in a decision tree, enforce sparsity (few non-zero weights) in linear models.
  • Enables higher-performing architectures (e.g., deep models) to be constrained toward interpretability.
  • Limitation: These models may still underperform compared to unconstrained complex models.

Wu et al., "Beyond sparsity: Tree regularization of deep models for interpretability." AAAI 2018.
Rudin et al., "Interpretable machine learning: Fundamental principles and 10 grand challenges." 2021.

Concept Bottleneck Models

A class of concept-based models that force the model to first predict human-interpretable concepts, then use those concepts to make the final prediction. Enables intervention: a human can correct a concept prediction before the final decision is made.

Koh, Pang Wei, et al. "Concept bottleneck models." ICML/PMLR, 2020.

G

Explanations-in-the-Loop — TED Framework

Train AI systems to jointly provide a prediction and its explanation.

TED — Teaching Explanations for Decisions

Analogy: Like a supervisor training a new employee — show example situations, teach the correct action (approve/reject loan) and the reason ("insufficient salary"). At deployment, the employee makes independent decisions with explanations learned from supervision.

  • Training data: X (features), Y (labels), E (explanations = human-annotated rationales / ground-truth explanations).
  • Training: Learn to predict both Y and E from X using a generic classification model f.
  • Deployment: For a new instance, the model generates both the prediction Y and its explanation E simultaneously.

Hind et al., "TED: Teaching AI to Explain its Decisions." AIES 2019.

Datasets Annotated with Explanations

Goals of using explanation-annotated data: train better models via additional supervision, train interpretable self-explaining models, and evaluate plausibility by measuring agreement with human explanations. Types of rationales for text data:

  • Highlight: Mark part of the input (e.g., "What a wonderful day!" → Sentiment: positive).
  • Free text: Natural language explanation ("The answer is correct because the person spoke with a joyful voice").
  • Structured: Constrained form ("Is it joyful? Yes. Is it loud? Yes.").

Wiegreffe & Marasovic, "Teach Me to Explain." NeurIPS Benchmark and Datasets 2021.

Advantages
  • Explainability directly embedded in the training process.
  • Teaches the model what is important to humans.
  • Alignment to human reasoning and values.
  • Explanations can be tailored for the target audience.
Limitations
  • Requires a dataset annotated with explanations (expensive).
  • Explanations may reflect human expectations rather than actual model reasoning.
  • Faithfulness vs. Plausibility tension: Faithfulness = explanation matches inner model workings. Plausibility = explanation matches what humans expect.

Post-Modeling Explainability
Model-Agnostic & Global

A

Model-Agnostic Solutions

Post-hoc explainability generates explanations for pre-trained models. Model-agnostic methods treat the model as a black-box oracle — they use only its predictions and output probabilities, without accessing internal structure.

Advantages of Model-Agnostic Solutions
  • Model Flexibility / Compatibility: Can explain any complex, high-performing model. Works across different frameworks and libraries.
  • Explanation Flexibility: Adopt the explanation representation most suitable for target users and domains.
  • Representation Flexibility: The explanation representation (e.g., image patches, words) can differ from what the model uses internally (e.g., pixels, embeddings).
  • Lower Cost to Switch: The underlying model can be changed while preserving the explanation format.
  • Model Comparison: Easier to compare models when the explanation representation is consistent.

Ribeiro et al., "Model-agnostic interpretability of machine learning." arXiv 2016.

Global scope: how the model globally works  |  Subgroup scope: model behavior in data subgroups  |  Local scope: reasons behind individual predictions

B

Global Surrogate Model

An interpretable surrogate (proxy) trained to approximate the predictions of a complex black-box model. The surrogate g closely mimics the behavior of f, under the constraint that g is interpretable (decision tree, logistic regression, linear regression, rules).

Steps to Build a Global Surrogate
  • 1. Training Data X: Use the same dataset as the black-box model f, or a new dataset reflecting its distribution.
  • 2. Labeling: For dataset X, obtain the predictions of the black-box model f (not the ground truth labels).
  • 3. Choose Interpretable Model g: Select the model type that best suits the problem domain (tree, linear model, rules).
  • 4. Surrogate Training: Train g on X using f's predictions. g learns to approximate f's behavior.
  • 5. Evaluation: Measure how well g replicates f's predictions using MSE, accuracy, or AUC-ROC.
  • 6. Interpretation: Interpret g to gain insights into f's decision-making process.

christophm.github.io/interpretable-ml-book/global.html

Variation: TREPAN
  • Uses a decision tree as the interpretable surrogate g.
  • Considers fidelity to the original model during tree construction.
  • Best-first expansion: Prioritizes nodes with the greatest potential to increase fidelity. Node evaluation: reach(n) × (1 − fidelity(n))
  • reach(n): estimated fraction of instances that reach node n. fidelity(n): estimated fidelity of the tree to the network at node n.

Craven & Shavlik, "Extracting tree-structured representations of trained networks." NIPS 1995.

Advantages
  • Simplified representation of complex models.
  • Different explanation forms depending on the interpretable model chosen.
  • Can enable both global and local explainability.
  • Easy to build; model-agnostic for both the model to explain and the surrogate adopted.
  • Flexible choice of interpretable model g.
Limitations
  • Approximation: May fail to capture all complexity of f.
  • Oversimplification: Risk of oversimplifying decision boundaries.
  • Global behavior only: May not capture local nuances or specific decision boundaries.
  • Data dependence: Quality relies heavily on training data representativeness.
  • Interpretability: The surrogate itself can still be difficult to interpret.
C

Permutation Feature Importance

Estimates feature importance by measuring how much the model's performance decreases when the values of a feature are randomly shuffled. The larger the drop in performance, the more important the feature.

Algorithm
  • 1. Reference Score: Compute the model's baseline performance (e.g., accuracy) on dataset D.
  • 2. For each feature:
  • Permute: Randomly shuffle the feature's values across D (breaking its relationship with the target).
  • Evaluate: Apply the model on the permuted dataset and record the performance metric.
  • Importance Score: Difference (or ratio) between the reference performance and the permuted performance.
  • 3. Rank: Features are ranked by importance score; higher performance drop = higher importance.
  • 4. Repeat N times: Repeat permutations N times to account for randomness and improve stability.

Visualization: bar plot (mean importance) and box plot (distribution over N repetitions). Example: Titanic dataset.

Advantages
  • Model-agnostic.
  • Intuitive interpretation of feature importance.
  • Compressed, global insight into model behavior.
  • Simple to implement; no model retraining needed.
  • No assumption of linearity; captures complex, non-linear relationships.
  • Performance ratio enables comparison across different models and problems.
Limitations
  • Feature Correlation: If features are correlated, permuting one leaves the model access to the other → importance of correlated features is underestimated (shared). Creates unrealistic data instances (e.g., height 1.8m, weight 20kg).
  • Linked to model performance — other measures (e.g., variance explained) may also be of interest.
  • Requires ground truth labels.
  • Randomicity: shuffling introduces variance; repeating stabilizes but increases computation time.
D

Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs)

A visualization tool showing the relationship between model predictions and specific input features, marginalizing over all other features. Reveals how changes in a feature influence the target response.

Definition & Computation
  • S: Features of interest (typically 1 or 2 due to limits of human perception).
  • C: All other features (complement of S).
  • The partial dependence marginalizes the model output over C, showing only the relationship between S and the outcome.
  • Computation: For a given value xₐ of features S, replace the values of S in every data point with xₐ, run the model on all instances, and average the predictions: PD(xₐ) ≈ (1/n) ∑ f(xₐ, x₇ₐ̓̓)
  • Repeat for many values of xₐ and plot → the PDP curve.

Example (Titanic, class 1): Age=10 → average prediction 0.9475; Age=20 → average prediction 0.525. Plot shows declining survival probability with age.

Advantages
  • Intuitive computation and interpretation: "average prediction if we force all data points to assume that feature value."
  • Explanation in visualization form — easy to inspect.
  • Shows how average prediction changes as feature S is varied.
  • Easy to implement.
Limitations
  • Independence Assumption: Assumes no correlation between S and C. If correlated, unrealistic data instances are created during marginalization.
  • Typically analyzes only one feature at a time.
  • Does not show feature distribution → risk of overinterpreting sparse regions.
  • Average marginal effect may hide heterogeneous effects (e.g., positive and negative effects that cancel out).

Interpretable Models

Eliana Pastor · Explainable and Trustworthy AI

A

Overview — Local Surrogate Models

Explaining individual predictions by training an interpretable model locally — in the neighborhood of the instance to explain. Provides qualitative understanding of the relationship between the instance's components and the model's prediction.

From Global to Local Surrogate
  • Global surrogate: Approximates the black-box model f over the entire input space.
  • Local surrogate: Approximates f only in the locality of a specific prediction — a much simpler and more faithful task for that region.
  • Local fidelity does not imply global fidelity.
Two Key Properties of Local Explanations
  • Interpretable: Provide qualitative understanding, easy to interpret. Features used for explaining can differ from features used for training — notion of interpretable data representation.
  • Locally Faithful: Correspond to how the model actually behaves in the vicinity of the instance being explained — property of local fidelity.
B

LIME — Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations

Train a local interpretable model in the locality of the prediction, using interpretable representations and perturbed neighborhood samples.

Formal Definition

The explanation for instance x is the model g that minimizes:

explanation(x) = argming ∈ G L(f, g, πx) + Ω(g)

  • f: The black-box model to explain. x: Instance to explain.
  • G: Family of possible interpretable models.
  • πx: Proximity measure between x and perturbed instances z — defines locality.
  • L(f, g, πx): How unfaithful g is to f in the locality defined by πx — local fidelity.
  • Ω(g): Complexity of g (e.g., number of non-zero weights in a linear model) — kept low for interpretability.
High-Level Steps
  • 1. Generate the neighborhood of x: Create perturbed samples around x.
  • 2. Label with f: Get the predictions of the black-box model f for these local points.
  • 3. Weigh by proximity: Weight each sample according to its proximity to x.
  • 4. Train a weighted interpretable model: Train g (e.g., linear model) on the weighted neighborhood dataset.
  • 5. Explain: Interpret g to explain the prediction for x.
Point (a) — Interpretable Data Representations

Explanations use representations interpretable to humans, which can differ from model representations:

  • Text: Model uses embeddings → LIME uses individual words. Encoded as binary vector: 1 = word present, 0 = word removed.
  • Images: Model uses pixel values (W×H×C) → LIME uses superpixels/patches. Binary: 1 = patch present, 0 = patch occluded.
  • Tabular: Features are already interpretable → used directly as-is.
Point (b) — Generating the Locality / Neighborhood
  • Text: Randomly remove words from the input. Binary vector encodes presence/absence. Probability assigned by the original model (concatenate remaining words or replace removed ones with [UNK] tokens). Proximity: cosine similarity between perturbed and original instance.
  • Images: Randomly occlude superpixels (set to grey/black). Binary: which patches are present.
  • Tabular — Numerical: Perturb by sampling from Normal(0,1), then inverse-transform using training mean and std.
  • Tabular — Categorical: Perturb by sampling according to the training distribution. Represent as binary feature: 1 if same value as input (used to compute proximity).
Point (c) — Choice of Interpretable Model g
  • Train g on the generated neighborhood samples represented via interpretable representation.
  • Linear model with regularization: LASSO (L1, minimizes non-zero coefficients) or Ridge (L2 regularization).
  • Parameter K controls interpretability — e.g., for text: limit the number of words in the explanation. A feature selection step is applied first.
Advantages of LIME
  • Model-agnostic.
  • Local, instance-level explanations.
  • Interpretable representations (distinct from model representations; different abstraction levels).
  • Provides feature attributions.
  • Controllable number of interpretable features (shorter = more interpretable).
  • Supports multiple data types: images, text, tabular.
Disadvantages of LIME
  • Perturbed neighborhood samples may be unrealistic (ignores feature correlations).
  • Sensitive to perturbation method and magnitude.
  • Explanation instability: Results can differ across multiple runs (random perturbations).
  • Choice of neighborhood size and locality affects results.
  • Potential inconsistency: Similar instances can yield different explanations.
  • Dependence on the choice of interpretable representation.
C

LORE — Local Rule-Based Explanations

Definition
  • Local surrogate: Decision tree classifier trained in the neighborhood.
  • Neighborhood generation: Based on a genetic algorithm to generate realistic and diverse neighbors.
  • Explanation output:
    • → Decision path (local rule): the if–then rule leading to the prediction.
    • → Counterfactual rules: conditions that, if changed, would change the predicted class.

Guidotti et al., "Local rule-based explanations of black box decision systems." 2018.

Advantages
  • Model-agnostic.
  • Local explanations.
  • Provides local rules (human-readable).
  • Provides counterfactual explanations.
Limitations
  • Genetic neighborhood generation can be computationally expensive.
  • Generated samples may still be unrealistic.
  • Focused on structured/tabular data.
D

LACE — Local Associative Classifier Explanation

Definition
  • Local surrogate: Associative classifier trained on the actual neighborhood.
  • Neighborhood generation: Based on actual training data neighbors (not synthetic generation).
  • Explanation output:
    • → Association rule (local rule).
    • → Feature attributions as prediction differences for individual features and local rules.

Pastor, Eliana, and Elena Baralis. "Explaining black box models by means of local rules." SAC 2019.

Advantages
  • Model-agnostic.
  • Local explanations.
  • Provides local rules.
  • Provides prediction differences for individual features and local rules.
Limitations
  • Requires access to the actual training data to derive the neighborhood.
  • Neighborhood from training data may be insufficient to capture local behavior.
  • Focused on structured/tabular data.

Explaining by
Removing / Perturbing

Eliana Pastor · Explainable and Trustworthy AI

A

Overview — Explaining by Removing

Also known as occlusion-based or perturbation-based. Core idea: remove or simulate the removal of one or more input features to quantify their influence on the prediction.

◆ PredDiff — Base approach: direct prediction difference.
◆ IME — Explaining with Shapley Values (exact).
◆ SHAP — SHapley Additive exPlanations (efficient approximation + global views).
◆ LIME* — Also uses perturbations under the hood.
B

PredDiff — Prediction Difference

Definition

The importance of attribute Aᵢ for instance x:

importanceᵢ(x) = f(x) − f(x \ Aᵢ)

Where x \ Aᵢ is the instance x without the information of Aᵢ. Two ways to evaluate the difference:

  • Solution a) Difference in probability: importanceᵢ(x) = P(y=c | x) − P(y=c | x \ Aᵢ). Where c is typically the predicted class.
  • Solution b) Information difference: infoᵢ(x) = log₂ P(y=c | x) − log₂ P(y=c | x \ Aᵢ).
Simulating Feature Removal f(x \ Aᵢ)

Replace the feature value with a weighted average over all possible values:

  • Categorical attributes: Replace Aᵢ with all possible values, weight each prediction by the prior probability of that value.
  • Numerical attributes: First discretize Aᵢ into sub-intervals; use middle points as representative values and sub-interval probabilities as weights.

Result: positive contribution → attribute value pushes toward class c. Negative → pushes against class c. Higher magnitude → more impact.

Advantages
  • Model-agnostic.
  • Local explanations.
  • Provides feature attributions.
  • Direct interpretation of the result.
Limitations
  • Defined for structured/tabular data.
  • Perturbations may create unrealistic data instances.
  • Requires access to data to compute x \ Aᵢ.
  • Does not consider feature interactions for the removal.
C

Shapley Values

A concept from cooperative game theory: assign a relevance score to each player (feature) of a team (model), assuming they collaborate. Considers all possible feature interactions.

Formal Definition

n players. v maps subsets (coalitions) S to real numbers: v: 2ⁿ → ℝ, v(∅) = 0. The Shapley value of player i:

φᵢ(v) = ∑S ⊆ N\{i} [ |S|! (n−|S|−1)! / n! ] × (v(S ∪ {i}) − v(S))

The weighted average of player i's marginal contribution over all possible coalitions.

4 Axiomatic Properties (Unique)
  • Efficiency: ∑i∈N φᵢ(v) = v(N). The sum of all Shapley values equals the total value of the team.
  • Symmetry: Players with equal marginal contributions in all coalitions receive equal attribution.
  • Linearity: Shapley values distribute linearly over combined games: φᵢ(v+w) = φᵢ(v) + φᵢ(w).
  • Null Player: A player with zero marginal contribution in all coalitions gets attribution = 0.

Shapley value is the only assignment satisfying all four properties simultaneously.

Mapping to XAI
  • Players → feature values of the instance.
  • Total score v(N) → prediction minus average prediction (prior).
  • Coalition S → instance where feature values not in S are omitted.
  • v(S) → prediction probability when only features in S are available, compared to average prediction.
  • Output φ → feature attributions: how much each feature value contributed to the prediction vs. the average prediction.
  • Efficiency in XAI: ∑ φᵢ = f(x) − E[f(x)]. The prediction difference from average is fully distributed among features.

Example: x = {Gender=F, age=30, nationality=IT, income=20k}. To compute φ(Gender=F) we must evaluate all 2³ = 8 coalitions of the other 3 features, with and without Gender=F.

Computing v(S) — Simulating Removal
  • Average value substitution: Replace missing features with weighted average over possible values (as in PredDiff). Less commonly used.
  • Random substitution (most common): Replace missing features with random values sampled from the dataset. Run multiple replacements and average → reduces variance of the estimate.
Approximation via Monte-Carlo Sampling

Exact computation is exponential: 2ⁿ coalitions for n features. Solution: Monte-Carlo approximation (Strumbelj & Kononenko, 2014).

  • For m in 1…M iterations:
    • → Sample a random instance z from the dataset.
    • → Choose a random permutation O of feature values.
    • → Build x⁺ (features preceding j in O from x, j from x, features succeeding j from z).
    • → Build x⁻ (same but j-th also from z).
    • → Marginal contribution: f(x⁺) − f(x⁻).
  • Final estimate: φ̂ᵢ = (1/M) ∑ marginal contributions.
  • Higher M → lower variance, higher computation cost.
Advantages
  • Model-agnostic, local, feature attributions.
  • Considers feature interactions.
  • Efficiency property: prediction difference fully distributed.
  • Solid theoretical foundation (4 axioms).
Limitations
  • Exact computation is exponential in the number of features.
  • Requires data access to compute v(S).
  • Removal via random substitution creates unrealistic instances.
  • Approximation introduces variance.
D

SHAP — SHapley Additive exPlanations

Local, model-agnostic explanation method based on Shapley values. Represents the Shapley value explanation as an additive feature attribution method (a linear model). Also provides global insights by aggregating local explanations.

Additive Feature Attribution Framework

x: original instance. x': simplified binary input (1 = feature present, 0 = absent). hₓ: mapping from x' back to original space.

g(x') = φ₀ + ∑i=1M φᵢ x'ᵢ

  • φ₀ = E[f(x)] — base value (average prediction, what we predict knowing no features).
  • φᵢ — Shapley value for feature i — its contribution to the prediction.
  • x'ᵢ ∈ {0,1} — binary: 1 if feature i is present in the coalition, 0 if absent.

Interpretable representation examples: Text → words (binary: word present/absent). Images → superpixels (binary: patch visible/occluded). Tabular → features directly.

3 Properties of Additive Feature Attributions
  • Local Accuracy: When x = hₓ(x'), the explanation model g(x') matches the original model f(x). The sum of all φᵢ equals the prediction minus the base value.
  • Missingness: Features that are absent (x'ᵢ = 0) receive attribution φᵢ = 0 — missing features have no impact.
  • Consistency: If a model changes so that a feature's marginal contribution increases, its attribution must not decrease.

Shapley value is the only additive feature attribution method satisfying all 3 properties.

KernelSHAP — Model-Agnostic Estimation

Estimates Shapley values using the LIME framework with a specific SHAP kernel weight — bridges LIME and Shapley values.

  • Sample K coalitions z' ∈ {0,1}ⁿ.
  • Map each z' to the original feature space via hₓ and compute f(hₓ(z')).
  • Compute the SHAP kernel weight: πₓ(z') = (M−1) / [C(M, |z'|) × |z'| × (M − |z'|)].
  • Fit a weighted linear model. The coefficients φᵢ are the (approximated) Shapley values.
  • Complexity: Ω(g) = 0 (no regularization penalty in the SHAP formulation).

Lundberg & Lee, "A unified approach to interpreting model predictions." NeurIPS 2017.

TreeSHAP — Efficient Tree-Specific Estimation

Not model-agnostic. Exploits the structure of tree-based models for efficient exact (or near-exact) Shapley computation. Much faster than KernelSHAP for random forests, gradient boosting, etc.

Global Insights from SHAP
  • Global Feature Importance: Average of absolute Shapley values per feature across all instances: I⸉ = (1/n) ∑ |φ⸉(i)|. Higher value → more globally important feature.
  • Summary Plot (Density Scatter): One point per (instance, feature) pair. x-axis = Shapley value, y-axis = feature. Features sorted by sum of |SHAP| magnitudes. Color encodes feature value — shows direction and distribution of impact.
  • Dependence Plot: For a feature j, plot {feature value, Shapley value} for each instance. Color by another feature k to highlight interaction effects.
Advantages of SHAP
  • All Shapley advantages (model-agnostic, local, feature attributions, interactions, solid theory).
  • Reduced computational complexity vs. exact Shapley.
  • Global model interpretations via aggregation.
  • Well-documented, actively maintained library.
Limitations of SHAP
  • Requires data access to compute f(hₓ(z')).
  • Feature removal via random substitution → unrealistic instances.
  • Assumes feature independence.
  • Approximation of Shapley values → variance.
  • KernelSHAP is slow (faster variants exist in the library).
E

Unified Framework for Removal-Based Explanations

All removal-based methods share the same core principle. They can be characterized along three dimensions:

Dimension 1 — Feature Removal f(x \ S̅)
  • Zeroing: Set removed features to zero: f(xₐ, 0).
  • Default Values: Replace with user-defined defaults (e.g., gray pixels for images, mean values as in LIME for images): f(xₐ, d̅).
  • Blurring: Remove image features by blurring with a Gaussian kernel.
  • Marginalize with marginal: Marginalize out using joint marginal distribution (e.g., KernelSHAP): E[f(xₐ, x̅̅)].
Dimension 2 — Model Behavior Explained
  • Prediction probability: f(x) = P(y=c | x).
  • Prediction loss: How much the loss increases when features are removed.
  • Dataset loss: Expected loss across the entire dataset — quantifies global performance degradation.
Dimension 3 — Summarizing Feature Influence
  • Remove individual: Impact of removing a single feature (e.g., PredDiff). Applicable also to interpretable representations (e.g., superpixels).
  • Additive model: Fit a regularized linear model to perturbed examples (e.g., LIME). Coefficients = incremental value of including each feature.
  • Shapley value: Feature attributions using the full Shapley framework (e.g., SHAP, IME).

Common limitations of all removal-based methods: (1) Perturbations may create unrealistic instances. (2) Feature absence is often simulated via marginalization/random values → requires data access and introduces variance.

Covert, Lundberg & Lee, "Explaining by removing: A unified framework." JMLR 2021.

Gradient-Based
Explainability Methods

Eliana Pastor · Explainable and Trustworthy AI

A

Overview — Gradient-Based Methods

Core Idea
  • Leverage gradient information of the model with respect to input features to identify which features are most influential in the decision-making process.
  • Methods differ in how the gradient is computed.
  • Explanation has the same size as the input — assigns each input element a relevance score (e.g., per-pixel importance for images, per-token importance for text).
Saliency Maps
  • Visualization technique to highlight the most important regions or features in an input sample.
  • Represent attribution scores assigned to each input feature visually.
  • Brighter regions = higher saliency = more important for the prediction.
  • Applicable to images (pixel-level), text (token-level), and structured data.
B

Vanilla Gradient

Definition

Compute the gradient of the class score with respect to the input features (originally proposed for image pixels). Key distinction from training-time backprop:

  • Gradients at training time: Computed w.r.t. model parameters to update weights. Indicate how parameter changes affect the loss.
  • Gradients for explainability: Computed w.r.t. input features. Indicate how input changes affect the model output directly.

Given a network with F(x) = [F₁(x), …, F₂(x)] output vector and class score F,(x) for class c, the gradient importance for all features:

∇, F,(x) = [∂F,/∂x₁, …, ∂F,/∂xₘ]

Interpretation: features with larger gradients require the smallest change to affect the class score the most. The gradient approximates F, locally as a linear function via first-order Taylor expansion: F,(x) ≈ w · x + b, where w = ∇F, defines feature importance.

From Gradient to Saliency Map

For images x ∈ ℝW×H×K (K=3 for RGB), gradient w has the same shape. To get a 2D saliency map M ∈ ℝW×H:

Mᵢ⸉ = max⁽k⁾ |wᵢ⸉⁽k⁾|

Collapse the channel dimension by taking the max absolute value across channels for each pixel location.

Limitation — Noisy Gradients

Vanilla gradients are noisy: small variations in the input can cause significant fluctuations in the gradient, resulting in unstable and hard-to-interpret saliency maps.

C

SmoothGrad

Definition

Reduces noise by averaging gradients over N perturbed (noisy) versions of the input:

SmoothGrad(x) = (1/N) ∑ ∇F,(x + ε)    where ε ~ N(0, σ²)

  • Generate N versions of the input by adding Gaussian noise.
  • Compute gradients for each noisy input → N relevance score maps.
  • Average the pixel attribution maps → smoothed saliency map.
  • Key parameters: noise level σ and number of samples N.
  • Intuition: averaging over perturbed inputs smooths out local fluctuations and averages out noise.
  • Can be applied on top of any gradient-based method, not only Vanilla Gradient.
D

Gradient × Input

Definition

A small variation of Vanilla Gradient: multiply the gradient element-wise by the input itself.

Gradient × Input = ∇F,(x) ⊙ x

Generally produces sharper and better results than Vanilla Gradient. Can also be combined with SmoothGrad: (Gradient × Input) + SmoothGrad.

⚠ Limitation: Gradient × Input fails the sensitivity axiom. Example: f(x) = 1 − ReLU(1−x). f(0) = 0, f(2) = 1 — different outputs, but InputXGradient gives 0 for both inputs → attribution is insensitive to this difference.

E

GradCAM — Gradient-Weighted Class Activation Mapping

Suitable for CNN-based architectures. Instead of backpropagating gradients all the way to the input pixels, GradCAM propagates to the last convolutional layer.

Intuition
  • Deeper CNN representations capture higher-level visual constructs.
  • Convolutional layers retain spatial information lost in fully-connected layers.
  • The last convolutional layer offers the best compromise: high-level semantics + spatial detail.
  • GradCAM uses gradient information flowing into the last convolutional layer to assign importance to each neuron/channel for a specific decision.
3-Step Process
  • Step a — Compute gradients: Backpropagate the gradient of class score y, w.r.t. feature map activations A⁾ of the last convolutional layer: ∂y, / ∂Aᵢ⸉⁾.
  • Step b — Global average pooling: Average gradients over all spatial locations for each channel k: α⁾, = (1/Z) ∑ᵢ ∑⸉ (∂y, / ∂Aᵢ⸉⁾). This captures the importance of feature map k for class c.
  • Step c — Weighted combination + ReLU: GradCAM, = ReLU(∑⁾ α⁾, · A⁾). ReLU keeps only features with positive influence on the class (pixels whose intensity increase boosts class c).

Output: a coarse localization heatmap of size W×H (same as feature maps). Typically upsampled to match input image size and used as a mask overlay.

Guided GradCAM — Per-Pixel Refinement

GradCAM produces coarse heatmaps because the last convolutional feature maps have lower resolution than the input. To achieve per-pixel importance:

Guided GradCAM = Upsampled GradCAM, ⊙ Vanilla Gradient,

Element-wise product combines the spatial localization of GradCAM with the fine-grained pixel attribution of Vanilla Gradient.

F

Integrated Gradients

Proposed with two axioms that a proper attribution method should satisfy. Addresses the sensitivity failure of Gradient × Input.

Two Axioms
  • Sensitivity: If two inputs x and x' differ only in one feature xᵢ, but have different predictions, then xᵢ must receive a non-zero attribution. (Gradient × Input fails this — shown by counterexample with f(x) = 1 − ReLU(1−x).)
  • Implementation Invariance: If two models f and f' have identical input/output behavior (are functionally equivalent), then their attributions must be identical regardless of internal implementation differences.
Definition & Computation

Compare x against a baseline x' (no-information input, e.g., zero vector or black image). Interpolate between x' and x, compute gradients at each interpolated point, and integrate:

IGᵢ(x) = (xᵢ − x'ᵢ) × ∫₀¹ (∂F(x' + α(x−x')) / ∂xᵢ) dα

Numerical approximation with N steps:

IGᵢapprox(x) = (xᵢ − x'ᵢ) × (1/N) × ∑k=1N ∂F(x' + (k/N)(x−x')) / ∂xᵢ

5-Step Process
  • 1. Define a baseline x' (e.g., zero vector, black image).
  • 2. Interpolate between x' and x at N steps: x' + (k/N) × (x − x') for k = 1…N.
  • 3. Compute the gradient at each interpolated input.
  • 4. Average the gradients (approximation of the integral).
  • 5. Scale by (xᵢ − x'ᵢ) to remain in the original space.

Intuition: total contribution of each input feature as we move from "nothing" (baseline) to the actual input. Satisfies both Sensitivity and Implementation Invariance axioms.

Sundararajan, Taly & Yan, "Axiomatic attribution for deep networks." ICML/PMLR 2017.

Sensitivity Proof on f(x) = 1 − ReLU(1−x)
  • f(0) = 0, f(2) = 1 → different outputs, sensitivity axiom requires different attributions.
  • IG(x=2, x'=0) ≈ 1  ✓   IG(x=0, x'=0) ≈ 0  ✓
  • Integrated Gradients correctly assigns different attributions — passes the sensitivity test.
  • Can also be combined with SmoothGrad for further noise reduction (at higher computational cost).
G

General Advantages & Limitations

Advantages
  • Efficiency: Many gradient-based methods are computationally efficient (single backpropagation pass).
  • Multiple approaches: Wide range of techniques for different architectures and needs.
  • Effective visualization: Saliency maps provide intuitive, pixel-level explanations.
  • Same resolution as input: Attribution scores map directly onto input elements.
Limitations
  • Sensitivity axiom failures: Some methods (e.g., Gradient × Input) do not satisfy sensitivity.
  • Insensitivity to model/data: Risk that explainers act as edge detectors rather than true model explainers.
  • Sensitivity to perturbations: Small input changes can lead to unstable explanations (vanilla gradient).
  • Gradient vanishing/saturation: Deep networks may produce uninformative gradients.
  • Method disagreement: Different methods yield different explanations → need for evaluation approaches to decide which to trust.

Method summary: Vanilla Gradient → SmoothGrad (noise reduction) → Gradient×Input (sharpness) → GradCAM (spatial, CNN-specific) → Guided GradCAM (per-pixel) → Integrated Gradients (axiomatic, most theoretically sound).

From Anecdotal Evidence to Systematization

The Problem with Anecdotal Evaluation
  • Evaluating explanations by showing convincing examples — "seems valid, plausible to us as humans, seems clear."
  • Does not allow systematic, quantifiable, or comparable analysis of explanation quality.
  • A systematization is necessary for ensuring quality and trustworthiness in real applications.
Systematization Goals
  • Define properties of explanation quality.
  • Define evaluation methods and measures to quantify those properties.

Reference: Nauta et al., "From anecdotal evidence to quantitative evaluation methods: A systematic review on evaluating explainable AI." ACM Computing Surveys 55.13s (2023): 1–42.

Key Distinction: Faithfulness vs. Plausibility
  • Plausibility: Alignment of the explanation with human reasoning — what we expect as humans.
  • Faithfulness: Alignment of the explanation with the model's actual behavior — its inner workings.
  • We cannot assume that explanations are by default faithful. Explainers may fail — we must test them.
  • There is no guarantee that a plausible explanation is also faithful, and vice versa.
  • A non-plausible explanation may indicate either a model reasoning error or an explainer error.

Content / Model Properties

Faithfulness

Alignment with the model's inner workings. "Is the explanation reflecting the behavior of the model?" Two sub-properties:

  • Correctness / Comprehensiveness: Whether the explanation captures all elements relevant for the model's outcome.
  • Completeness / Sufficiency: Whether the set of elements highlighted is sufficient to explain the model's output.
Consistency
  • Identical inputs must produce identical explanations — assesses how deterministic the explanation method is.
  • Implementation Invariance: Two models that give the same outputs for all inputs must also have the same explanations.
Continuity
  • Similar inputs should produce similar explanations.
  • Describes how smooth/continuous the explanation function is.
  • For small input perturbations, we expect both similar model responses and similar explanations.
Contrastivity
  • Describes how discriminative the explanation is with respect to other targets or events.
  • An explanation should explain not only why a prediction was made, but also why not other outcomes.
  • Separability: Non-identical instances from different classes must have dissimilar explanations.
Covariate Complexity
  • Complexity of the covariates (features) used in the explanation.
  • Features should be comprehensible — use interpretable data representations.
  • Often used for Concept-based XAI: Covariate Homogeneity — how consistently a concept (e.g., a prototype) represents a single predefined human-interpretable concept. Higher disentanglement = better.
D

Presentation Properties

Compactness
  • Size of the explanation — motivated by the limits of human cognitive capacity.
  • Explanations should be sparse, short, and non-redundant. The more compact, the more understandable.
  • Measured as: number of features in the explanation, length of the rule/decision path.
  • Redundancy: The lower the overlap among explanations, the higher the interpretability.
Composition
  • Describes the presentation format, organization, and structure of the explanation.
  • Focus on how the prediction/model is explained — prefer clear, higher-level forms.
  • Different forms may be preferred based on target users (data scientist, domain expert, policy maker).
  • Often evaluated via anecdotal evidence or user studies.
Confidence
  • Whether the explanation includes a measure of uncertainty.
  • Few methods currently assess this aspect.
E

User Properties

Plausibility / Coherence
  • Alignment of the explanation with human reasoning, background knowledge, beliefs, and general consensus. Also known as reasonableness or agreement with human rationales.
  • Evaluation via user studies: Human assessment of explanation quality.
  • Evaluation via annotated datasets: Compare explanations against human-annotated ground truth rationales. Metrics: rank correlation (feature importance), Intersection-over-Union (saliency maps), ROUGE/BLEU (textual explanations).
  • XAI Method Agreement: Compare a novel explainer with an established one with known properties.
Context
  • How relevant the explanation is to the user and their specific needs.
  • Explanations should be designed based on the stakeholder type: data scientist, data controller, domain expert, policy maker.
  • Expertise level of the target user must be considered.
  • Mostly assessed with user studies.
Controllability
  • How much the user can control, correct, or interact with an explanation.
  • Mostly assessed with user studies or via anecdotal evidence.

Methods for Evaluating Faithfulness

I — Removal-Based Evaluation

Study the effect of removing/perturbing what the explanation highlights and measure the impact on model output f. Used for feature attribution methods. Problem: as with removal-based explanations, may produce out-of-distribution samples.

  • Single Deletion: Remove one feature. Omitting the highest-importance feature should cause the largest output change. Omitting the lowest-importance feature should have no impact. A feature with no effect on the output should have importance = 0.
  • Incremental Deletion: Iteratively remove features in descending importance order (most → least important). Summarize impact via Area over the Perturbation Curve or average prediction difference.
    • → Comprehensiveness (Correctness): Remove top-k% most important features. Expect high prediction drop. Higher = better. Filter out negatively contributing features first.
    • → Sufficiency (Completeness): Remove bottom-k% least important features (keep only important ones). Expect small or zero prediction drop. Closer to 0 = better.
  • Incremental Addition: Iteratively add features starting from an empty input; observe how prediction changes as more features are included.
II — Sanity Checks

Model Parameter Randomization Check: Measure sensitivity of the explanation to the model f.

  • Compare the explanation of f with the explanation when model parameters are randomized / weights re-initialized.
  • We expect a change in the explanation after randomization.
  • If there is no change → the explanation is not sensitive to f → does not reflect the model's reasoning → explanation fails the sanity check.
III — White Box Check
  • Use an interpretable (white-box) model to derive ground-truth explanations.
  • Apply the explanation method to the white-box model and compare with the known ground-truth explanation.
  • Evaluate how closely the explanation reflects the true model reasoning.
IV — Synthetic Data Check
  • Train a model on controlled synthetic data with known ground-truth patterns (e.g., "if attribute=1, then class=1").
  • Compare the model's explanation with the known ground-truth explanation.
  • Note: assumes the model has learned the intended reasoning from the controlled data.
V — Fidelity
  • Agreement between the output of f and the explanation when the explanation is used to make predictions.
  • Use the explanation itself as a predictor (e.g., apply surrogate model or use feature weights to build a linear model).
  • Measure the fraction of samples for which f and the explanation make the same decision.
  • Different from Comprehensiveness/Sufficiency: Fidelity compares outputs, not the reasoning process.
G

Evaluating Consistency, Continuity & Contrastivity

Consistency — Implementation Invariance

Two models that give the same outputs for all inputs must produce the same explanations. Evaluate as similarity between feature importance scores across random re-initializations of f with the same architecture.

Continuity — Stability / Robustness

Measure similarity between the explanation for x and its slightly perturbed version (neighbor sample or noise-added version). Metrics: rank order correlation, cosine similarity between attribution vectors.

Contrastivity — Target Sensitivity

Evaluate the extent to which features highlighted for a certain class differ from features highlighted for other classes. The explanation for class c should differ from the explanation for class c'. Metric: compute similarity between explanations for x w.r.t. different classes — the larger the difference, the better.

Motivation — Why Standard XAI Falls Short

Problem 1 — Saliency Maps Are Hard to Compare
  • It is not easy to assess which explanation method is better by only looking at saliency maps.
  • Edge detectors produce similar explanations to some saliency methods (especially those that consider input values, e.g., Gradient × Input) — raising the question: are we explaining the model or detecting edges?

Adebayo et al., "Sanity checks for saliency maps." NeurIPS 2018.

Problem 2 — Sensitivity to Randomization is Weak
  • Randomizing a few layers has almost no effect on the explanation.
  • Even the explanation of a completely randomized network is still similar to the original one.
  • It is difficult to determine which layer is being randomized from the saliency map.
  • Conclusion: some explanation methods are more input-dependent than model-dependent — they do not truly reflect the model's reasoning.
Problem 3 — Class Ambiguity
  • Saliency maps of very different classes can still look similar.
  • It is difficult to determine which class is being explained just by looking at the map.

Rudin, "Stop explaining black box machine learning models." Nature Machine Intelligence (2019).

Core Insight

"Showing where a network is looking does not tell us what the network is seeing in a given input."

Rudin (2019); Achtibat et al., "From attribution maps to human-understandable explanations." Nature Machine Intelligence (2023).

Solution: move from pixel-level attributions to human-interpretable concepts.

Concept-Based XAI — What Is a Concept?

"A concept can be any abstraction, such as a colour, an object, or even an idea."

Molnar, "Interpretable machine learning." 2020.

Type 1 — Symbolic Concepts
  • Human-defined attributes or abstractions (e.g., "beak" for birds, "color of the bird").
  • Require auxiliary data and annotations. Two levels of annotation cost:
    • → Image-level: annotate concept presence for each individual image. More expensive, more precise.
    • → Class-level: all samples of a class are annotated as having a certain attribute. Less expensive, less precise (concept may not be visible in every image).
Type 2 — Unsupervised Concept Basis
  • Clusters of similar samples extracted from the model's latent space.
  • Not built to resemble human-defined concepts, but still capture abstractions more understandable than individual pixels.
  • Example: a cluster of green birds.
  • Requires clustering algorithms applied to the latent representation.
Type 3 — Prototypes
  • Explanation by example: representative examples of peculiar traits in the training data.
  • Can be entire samples or parts of a training sample (e.g., a particular type of beak).
  • The prototype set should be representative of the whole dataset.
  • Different from unsupervised bases: represents a single example rather than a group.
Type 4 — Textual Concepts
  • Textual descriptions of main classes, decomposed into distinctive characteristic pieces.
  • Each piece embodies a characteristic of the class; can be shared among classes (e.g., "bright feathers").
  • Generated at training time by an external LLM with knowledge of the task.
  • Employed in the form of numerical embeddings of the corresponding text.
C

Three Types of Concept-Based Explanations

1 — Class-Concept Relations
  • Relationship between a specific concept and an output class of the model.
  • Expressed as concept importance scores or logic rules involving multiple concepts and their connection to an output class.
  • Applicable to all concept types. E.g., with prototypes: parrot := 0.8 × prototype₁ + 0.2 × prototype₂.
2 — Node-Concept Association
  • Assign a concept to an internal unit (filter/neuron) of a network.
  • Enhances transparency by highlighting what internal units see in a given sample.
  • Post-hoc: Identify hidden units that maximally activate on samples representing a concept.
  • By design: Force a unit to predict a specific concept during training.
3 — Concept Visualization
  • Highlight the input features that best represent a specific concept — like a saliency map but for concepts.
  • Crucial when non-symbolic concepts (unsupervised bases, prototypes) are used.
  • Helps understand which unsupervised attributes or prototypes the network has learned.
  • Often combined with Class-Concept Relations or Node-Concept Association.
D

Post-Hoc vs. Explainable-By-Design C-XAI

Post-Hoc C-XAI
  • Pipeline: Take pre-trained model → project concept samples into latent space → analyze relationship with predictions or hidden activations.
  • Concepts can be supervised or unsupervised (prototypes and generative concepts less common).
  • Pro: Does not compromise the model's learning capacity.
  • Pro: More interpretable than standard post-hoc (pixel-level) methods.
  • Con: Cannot ensure the network truly knows the concepts — it was not trained for them.
Explainable-By-Design C-XAI
  • Pipeline: Neural models with an explicit concept representation as an intermediate layer. Predicted concepts influence task predictions.
  • All concept types and explanation forms can be used.
  • Pro: Inherently transparent — node-concept association by design.
  • Con: Requires ad-hoc training.
  • Con: Predicting concepts may reduce task performance.

C-XAI Taxonomy — Six Categories

Post-Hoc Supervised — Class-Concept Relations
  • Take a pre-trained model. Require data annotated with concepts.
  • Project concept samples into the model latent space. Correlate their projection with output class representations.
  • Kim et al., "Interpretability beyond feature attribution: TCAV." ICML 2018.
Post-Hoc Supervised — Node-Concept Association
  • Take a pre-trained model. Require data annotated with concepts.
  • Analyze activations of hidden nodes when fed concept samples. Associate each node with the concept that maximally activates it (on average).
  • Bau et al., "Network dissection: Quantifying interpretability of deep visual representations." CVPR 2017.
Post-Hoc Unsupervised — Class-Concept Relations
  • Take a pre-trained model. No concept annotations required.
  • Split input data into smaller crops → project into latent space → cluster projections (clusters = unsupervised concepts) → analyze correlation with output classes.
  • Ghorbani et al., "Towards automatic concept-based explanations." NeurIPS 2019.
By-Design Supervised — Joint Training (CBM-style)
  • Train from scratch with a hidden layer predicting annotated concepts. Node-concept association by design.
  • Predicted concepts used to make the final class prediction. If the task predictor is white-box → extract class-concept relations too.
  • Pro: Intuitive explanation ("I see a beak, feathers, no muzzle → bird"). Allows concept interventions — human can correct a concept at inference time.
  • Con: Requires data annotated with both classes and concepts.
  • Koh et al., "Concept bottleneck models." ICML 2020.
By-Design Supervised — Instilling Concepts
  • Take a pre-trained black-box model. Fine-tune a specific layer to predict given concepts → turns black-box into explainable-by-design.
  • Concept annotation data may differ from original training data. Top of the network continues to predict original classes.
  • Chen et al., "Concept whitening for interpretable image recognition." Nature Machine Intelligence 2020.
By-Design Unsupervised — Prototype Concepts
  • No concept annotations required. Train the network to predict output class and encode most representative training examples in hidden layers.
  • Node-concept association + class-concept relations (if white-box task predictor).
  • To visualize prototypes: find the (part of the) sample for which a prototype activates most.
  • Chen et al., "This looks like that." NeurIPS 2019.
By-Design Unsupervised — Unsupervised Concept Basis
  • Train the network to predict output class and create clusters in the latent representation.
  • Characterize concepts by visualizing samples closest to cluster centroids, or by decoding centroids with an autoencoder.
  • Alvarez Melis & Jaakkola, "Towards robust interpretability with self-explaining neural networks." NeurIPS 2018.
By-Design Hybrid Models
  • Train a subset of neurons to predict supervised concepts and create a clustered representation in the remaining neurons.
  • Pros: Overcomes accuracy trade-off of fully supervised models. Reduces annotation cost. Avoids concept leakage.
  • Cons: Most classification information encoded in unsupervised neurons → concept interventions are less effective.
Generative Concept-Based Models
  • Use an LLM to generate concept labels per class → decompose into small concept pieces → align corresponding text embeddings to the latent input representation → concept scores used for final classification.
  • Pro: No concept labelling by humans required.
  • Con: Per-class labelling only. Requires an external generative model with domain knowledge.
  • Yang et al., "Language in a bottle." IEEE CVPR 2023.

T-CAV — Testing with Concept Activation Vectors

Post-hoc supervised method providing class-concept relations. Goal: quantitatively measure how important user-chosen concepts are for a given class prediction — going beyond "where is the model looking" to "what is the model seeing."

5 Components of T-CAV
  • (a) A dataset annotated with examples of concepts and random images.
  • (b) The dataset with the original task classes.
  • (c) The pre-trained model to explain.
  • (d) The Concept Activation Vectors (CAVs).
  • (e) The TCAV score — showing the influence of a concept on a given class.

Kim et al., "Interpretability beyond feature attribution: TCAV." ICML 2018.

Step 1 — Defining CAVs (Concept Activation Vectors)
  • Feed concept images (e.g., stripe images) and random images into the pre-trained model.
  • Extract the internal tensors (activations) at layer l for both sets.
  • Train a linear classifier to separate the concept projections from the random image projections in the latent space.
  • The CAV v,ˡ is the vector orthogonal to the decision boundary of this linear classifier — it points in the direction of the concept in the latent space.
  • Sorting images with CAVs: Given a set of class images, compute cosine similarity between fˡ(x) (latent representation) and v,ˡ (CAV). Higher similarity → image is more representative of the concept.
Step 2 — Computing the TCAV Score
  • Compute the directional derivative of the class score for class k w.r.t. the CAV direction: S,,ₖ,ˡ(x).
  • S,,ₖ,ˡ(x) > 0 → positive influence of concept C on class k at layer l for instance x.
  • S,,ₖ,ˡ(x) < 0 → negative influence.
  • The TCAV score = fraction of class k samples with a positive directional derivative w.r.t. the CAV:

    TCAV,,ₖ,ˡ ∈ [0, 1]

  • TCAV > 0.5 → concept C has positive influence on class k. TCAV < 0.5 → negative influence.
Examples
  • Zebra: TCAV for "stripe" concept on class "zebra" → high positive score → stripes are indeed important for classifying zebras. ✓
  • Doctor (bias detection): TCAV for "woman" concept on class "doctor" → negative score → the model associates "doctor" more with male images → reveals gender bias in the model.
When & Where Are Concepts Learned?
  • Accuracy of the linear probe (linear classifier trained on concept vs. random): high accuracy → the network has automatically learned the concept in that layer. Low → the network does not use that concept.
  • Simpler concepts (e.g., colors, textures) have high linear probe accuracy throughout all layers.
  • High-level concepts (e.g., "stripe", "beak") are best detected at higher (later) layers.
B

CBM — Concept Bottleneck Models

Explainable-by-design supervised model. Unlike post-hoc methods, CBMs force the model to explicitly predict human-interpretable concepts as an intermediate step before making the final prediction.

Architecture
  • Concept Encoder g: Maps input x → concept predictions ĉ = g(x). Trained with concept loss L,(g(xᵢ); cᵢ).
  • Task Predictor f: Maps predicted concepts ĉ → final output ŷ = f(ĉ). Trained with task loss Ly(f(cᵢ); yᵢ).
  • The concept bottleneck layer is the explicit concept representation ĉ — a human-readable intermediate layer.
  • If f is a white-box model (e.g., linear model) → class-concept relations are directly readable from its weights.

Koh et al., "Concept bottleneck models." ICML 2020.

4 Training Strategies
  • Independent: Train g and f separately. g minimizes concept loss; f is trained on ground-truth concepts c. Most trustworthy — no concept leakage. f never sees x directly.
  • Sequential: Train g first (concept loss), freeze g, then train f on g(x). Also trustworthy — f never accesses x directly. Similar guarantees to Independent.
  • Joint: Train f and g together with a combined loss: Ly(f(cᵢ), yᵢ) + λ · L,(g(xᵢ), cᵢ). Better task accuracy but risk of concept leakage. Trade-off controlled by λ.
  • Standard (baseline): Train f and g end-to-end on task loss only — ignores concept loss. Highest task accuracy but not concept-supervised. Benchmark comparison only.

Only by explicitly training a model on concepts (CBM) can we ensure it represents all concepts. T-CAV (post-hoc probe) may miss concepts the model has not learned automatically.

Concept Intervention
  • At inference time, a domain expert can correct a wrongly predicted concept before the task predictor makes the final decision.
  • Example (X-ray): model predicts "no bone spur" → radiologist corrects to "bone spur present" → task prediction updates accordingly.
  • This is a powerful feature unique to by-design concept models: human-in-the-loop at concept level.
CBM Advantages
  • Explicit, human-readable concept representation.
  • Ensures the model learns all annotated concepts.
  • Supports concept interventions.
  • Node-concept association by design.
CBM Drawbacks
  • Poor accuracy trade-off: Struggles to balance accuracy and explainability.
  • Low concept efficiency: Does not scale well to real-world settings where concepts are scarce.
  • Requires data annotated with both classes and concepts.
C

CEM — Concept Embedding Models

Extends CBMs by replacing scalar concept scores with rich embedding vectors, overcoming the accuracy-explainability trade-off.

CEM Workflow — 5 Steps
  • 1. h = ψ(x): extract latent representation of the input.
  • 2. cᵢ⁺ = φᵢ⁺(x): a dedicated neural model produces the positive concept embedding for concept i (embedding when concept is present).
  • 3. pᵢ = s([cᵢ⁺, cᵢ⁻]): the concept score (probability of presence of concept i) is computed from the concatenation of positive and negative embeddings via a shared function s.
  • 4. ĉᵢ = pᵢ · cᵢ⁺ + (1 − pᵢ) · cᵢ⁻: the final concept embedding is a weighted combination of positive and negative embeddings, weighted by presence probability.
  • 5. f([ĉ₁, …, ĉᵢ, …, ĉₖ]): the task predictor operates on the concatenation of all concept embeddings.

Espinosa Zarlenga et al., "Concept embedding models: Beyond the accuracy-explainability trade-off." NeurIPS 2022.

Neural-Symbolic Spectrum
Neural
cᵢ ∈ ℝᵏ (unsupervised embeddings)
CEM
cᵢ ∈ ℝᵏ (supervised pairs: c⁺, c⁻)
CBM
cᵢ ∈ [0,1] (supervised scalars)

CEM sits between fully neural (opaque) and fully symbolic (CBM). Concepts are represented as pairs of rich supervised embeddings (positive + negative), giving more expressive power than a scalar probability.

CEM Advantages over CBM
  • Beyond the accuracy-explainability trade-off: CEMs retain high task accuracy while remaining concept-based.
  • High concept efficiency: Scales to real-world conditions where concept supervision is scarce.
  • Effective interventions: CEMs remain responsive to concept interventions (changing the predicted concept score actually affects the task prediction).
CEM vs. Hybrid Approach (CBM + Unsupervised Neurons)
  • Hybrid pros: Retains high accuracy. Has high concept efficiency similar to CEM. Reduces annotation cost.
  • Hybrid cons: Concept interventions have no effect — all task-relevant information is encoded in unsupervised neurons, so changing the concept score does not change the prediction.
Open Challenge — Interpretability of CEM
  • CBM: Interpretable — concept scores are scalars directly readable by humans.
  • CEM: Not directly interpretable — concept embeddings are high-dimensional vectors, not human-readable scalars.
  • Solution direction: Add an interpretable task predictor on top of CEM concept embeddings (e.g., "0.8 × Round + 0.1 × Red → Apple") to recover interpretability while retaining CEM's accuracy advantages.

What Is NLP?

Definition

An interdisciplinary field combining linguistics, computer science, and artificial intelligence, aiming to enable computers to understand, interpret, and generate human language in a meaningful and contextually relevant manner.

NLP Tasks
  • Classifying whole sentences: Sentiment Analysis, Spam Detection, Toxic Language Detection.
  • Classifying each word: Part-of-Speech Tagging, Named Entity Recognition (NER).
  • Generating text: Text Generation, Text Masking (fill-in-the-blank).
  • Extracting answers: Question Answering (given question + context, extract the answer).
  • Generating new sentences from input: Machine Translation, Text Summarization.
B

Word Representations

One-Hot Encoding (Traditional NLP) — Localist
  • Each word = a separate discrete symbol. Encoded as a one-hot vector of size = vocabulary size (e.g., 500k).
  • like = [0 0 0 … 1 … 0], love = [0 0 1 … 0 … 0].
  • Limitation 1: Huge, sparse vector dimensionality.
  • Limitation 2: No notion of similarity — all one-hot vectors are orthogonal (dot product = 0). "I like that movie" and "I love that movie" are treated as completely unrelated.
Word Embeddings — Distributed / Dense

Represent each word as a small, dense, real-valued vector. Core idea: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps" (Firth) — meaning is given by context (distributional semantics).

  • Fixed-size dimensionality (not vocabulary size). Similar words have similar vectors (measured by dot product or cosine similarity).
  • like = [0.34, −0.10, …, 0.22]  |  love = [0.29, −0.22, …, 0.29].
  • Vector arithmetic captures semantic relationships: king − man + woman ≈ queen.
  • Bias warning: Embeddings can inherit societal biases from training data. e.g., man − woman ≈ computer programmer − homemaker (Bolukbasi et al., NeurIPS 2016).
Word2Vec — Learning Embeddings
  • Start from a large text corpus. Select a fixed vocabulary; represent each word with a vector. Out-of-vocabulary words → [UNK].
  • For each center word, use nearby words as context. Maximize P(context word | center word) using similarity measures.
  • Adjust word vectors via gradient descent to maximize these probabilities.
  • Example: center word = "bark" → maximize P(dog|bark), P(when|bark), …
  • Mikolov et al. (2013). Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space. arXiv:1301.3781.
Limitations of Static Embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe)
  • Context-agnostic: Each word has one fixed vector regardless of context.
  • Polysemy problem: Words with multiple meanings get a single embedding. e.g., "river bank" vs. "savings bank" → same vector.
C

Language Modeling & Window-Based NNs

Language Modeling (LM)

Predict the next word given a sequence of words: given x⁽¹⁾, x⁽²⁾, …, x⁽ᵗ⁾, compute P(x⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ | x⁽¹⁾, …, x⁽ᵗ⁾).

Window-Based MLP — Limitations
  • Uses a fixed context window (e.g., 5 words). Words outside the window are discarded.
  • Limitation 1: Text can be arbitrarily long; fixed window misses distant dependencies.
  • Limitation 2: Increasing window size increases weight matrix size (W = window × embedding dim).
  • Limitation 3: Each word position uses completely different weights — no positional symmetry.
D

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)

Core Idea

Apply the same weights W repeatedly for each word (timestamp). Maintain a hidden state that is computed and fed back into the layer at each step — captures sequential context. Forward and backward propagation over time.

Sentence Encoding for Classification
  • Option 1 — Final hidden state: Use the last hidden state as the sentence encoding. Simple but may lose early context.
  • Option 2 — Element-wise mean/max of all hidden states: Aggregate all hidden states. Usually produces better results.
Two Improvements
  • Multi-layer RNNs: Stack multiple RNNs to create deeper representations (deep in the hidden dimension, not just in time).
  • Bidirectional RNNs: Process the sequence in both directions (left→right and right→left). Provides both left and right context. Only applicable when the full sequence is available (classification, translation) — not for language modeling.
RNN Architectures
  • One-to-one: Standard feed-forward (no sequence).
  • One-to-many: e.g., image captioning.
  • Many-to-one: e.g., sentence classification, sentiment analysis.
  • Many-to-many (same length): e.g., Named Entity Recognition, POS tagging.
  • Many-to-many (encoder-decoder): e.g., machine translation, sequence-to-sequence.
RNN Advantages
  • Process input text of any length.
  • Same weights W applied to each timestamp.
  • Can use past (and future in BiRNN) context.
  • Fixed model size — does not scale with input length.
RNN Limitations
  • Sequential: Forward/backward pass cannot be parallelized — slow on large corpora.
  • Vanishing/exploding gradients: Fail to learn long-distance dependencies. Interaction distance = O(sequence length).

Attention & Self-Attention

Attention Mechanism

Each word's representation serves as a query to access and incorporate information from a set of values to create new contextualized representations.

  • Better parallelization: Unparallelizable computations do not increase with sequence length.
  • Faster propagation: No sequential bottleneck.
  • O(1) interaction distance: All words attend to each other at every layer — no need for recurrency to interact.

Vaswani et al., "Attention is all you need." NeurIPS 2017.

Self-Attention
  • Functions as a fuzzy, soft lookup in a key-value store — weights are scores between 0 and 1 (not binary).
  • Output = weighted sum of all value vectors → creates a contextualized representation of each word.
  • Each word can attend to every other word in the same sequence (past and future) → "self" because query, key, and value all come from the same sequence.
3 Problems of Self-Attention & Solutions
  • Problem 1 — No order: Self-attention has no inherent notion of word order. → Solution: Add positional embeddings (learned absolute position representations) to the word embeddings at the first layer.
  • Problem 2 — No non-linearity: Stacking self-attention layers just re-averages value vectors (like stacking linear layers with no activation). → Solution: Add a feed-forward network after each self-attention block to post-process each output vector.
  • Problem 3 — Looking at the future: Self-attention attends to all words including future ones. This is fine for classification but is cheating for language modeling. → Solution: Mask future words by setting their attention scores to −∞ before softmax (masked self-attention).

Transformers

Architecture Overview
  • Based on multiple stacked attention blocks. Uses Multi-Head Attention: multiple attention heads running in parallel, each learning different aspects of relationships.
  • Each block: Multi-Head Attention → Add & Norm → Feed-Forward Network → Add & Norm.
Encoder
Uses self-attention (bidirectional). Creates contextualized bidirectional representations. Used for: classification, NER, encoding step of translation.
Decoder
Uses masked self-attention (unidirectional). Contextualizes only left context. Used for: language modeling, text generation.
Encoder-Decoder
Encoder processes source sentence bidirectionally. Decoder generates target sentence unidirectionally with cross-attention to encoder output. Used for: machine translation, summarization.

BERT — Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers

Overview
  • Uses only the encoder of the Transformer architecture (12 layers in BERT-base) → bidirectional representations.
  • A language model, but since it is encoder-only, it cannot do standard left-to-right language modeling. Two special pretraining tasks are introduced:
  • MLM — Masked Language Modeling: Randomly mask a fraction of tokens with [MASK], then predict the masked tokens. Forces the model to learn bidirectional context.
  • NSP — Next Sentence Prediction: Given two sentences, predict whether the second sentence follows the first in the original text.

Devlin et al., "BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding." NAACL 2019.

Input Representation
  • Token embeddings: Sub-token tokenization (e.g., "Sub##token") — handles unseen words by splitting into known sub-pieces.
  • Segment embeddings: Distinguish sentence A from sentence B.
  • Position embeddings: Encode word order.
  • [CLS] token: Special classification token prepended to the input. Its final hidden state is used as the sentence representation for classification tasks.
Pre-Training & Fine-Tuning Paradigm
  • Pre-training: Train BERT on a massive general corpus using MLM and NSP — learns rich language representations.
  • Fine-tuning: Add a small task-specific head (e.g., classification layer on top of [CLS]) and fine-tune the entire model on a labeled downstream dataset (sentiment analysis, NER, QA, etc.).
  • Pre-trained weights serve as a powerful starting point — significantly reduces data and computation needed for specific tasks.
